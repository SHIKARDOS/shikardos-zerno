# CHAPTER 7. FORMALIZATION OF THE 集 (COLLECTION) STAGE

## 7.1. The purpose of the collection stage

Collection is the first substantive stage of the Grain Principle. Its purpose is to give the subsequent synthesis material: not random associations and not the first solutions that come to mind, but systematically gathered specimens of how the task under study has already been solved — in different fields, in different eras, in different ways.

Collection is not preparatory, peripheral work. The quality of synthesis is directly bounded by the quality of collection: from a meagre or one-sided set one cannot build a solution surpassing what is already known. Therefore the collection stage is formalized as strictly as the rest.

The boundaries of collection are set by the task definition. Collection is always conducted relative to a concrete function or contradiction fixed at the previous step. One cannot collect "everything useful in general": such collection has no criterion of either inclusion or completion.

## 7.2. The three directions of search

Collection is organized along three directions that complement one another and cover different types of sources.

**The historical direction.** How was this task solved before? Here are collected the solutions found in the past — from early attempts to modern ones. The value of this direction is that it prevents the re-invention of the known and reveals the logic of development: how approaches changed, which constraints were lifted, which arose anew.

**The horizontal direction.** How is a similar function realized in adjacent fields? The task of transportation, amplification, storage, transformation, or feedback is solved in very different spheres — in technology, biology, organization, language. Horizontal search transfers principles between fields and is the main source of interdisciplinary novelty.

**The abstract direction.** Which general principles stand behind the found solutions? Here occurs the ascent from concrete specimens to general regularities — the lever, the wheel, feedback, redundancy — which can then be applied in a new form. The abstract direction binds collection to synthesis: it prepares the transition from a set of things to a set of functions.

The three directions are not strictly separate. One and the same grain may be discovered by several paths; the coincidence of paths only confirms its significance.

## 7.3. The "blind bee" rule: formalization

The blind-bee rule requires: the researcher collects solutions regardless of whether they please them, whether they correspond to their expectations, or whether they provoke inner resistance.

Formally the rule means the following. A solution is included in the collection not because it is attractive, habitual, or confirms the hypothesis, but because it is functionally relevant to the task and demonstrates a working mechanism. And conversely: a solution is not excluded merely because it is unfamiliar, contradicts the researcher's preferences, or comes from a field they do not regard as "their own".

The justification of the rule lies in the nature of novelty. The genuinely new arises most often at the intersection of fields and in a break with the habitual. A researcher who collects only what is close to them limits in advance the space of the possible by their own prejudices. The blind bee is a demand for intellectual honesty: to gather nectar, not confirmations.

Practically the rule is realized through the obligatory recording of what was consciously discarded: each exclusion must be written down together with its reason. This turns unconscious bias into an observable and verifiable act.

## 7.4. The collection procedure: steps

Collection is performed in the following sequence.

**Step 1. Fix the task.** From the task definition are written out the target function, the constraints, and the criteria by which the solution will be evaluated. Without this step collection is aimless.

**Step 2. Define the search space.** For each of the three directions the fields are marked in which the function may be realized: for the historical — preceding solutions in the given field; for the horizontal — adjacent disciplines; for the abstract — the general principles to which one may ascend.

**Step 3. Conduct the search.** In each marked field concrete specimens of solutions are identified. The search is documented: for each specimen the source is recorded.

**Step 4. Extract the grains.** From each specimen a functionally significant element — the grain — is singled out and formulated through the function it performs, not through outward form.

**Step 5. Record the discarded.** Everything that was considered but not included is written down with the reason for exclusion — in accordance with the blind-bee rule.

**Step 6. Check completeness.** Collection is recognized as sufficient when, along each of the three directions, specimens have been accumulated, and further search ceases to yield substantially new functions (see 7.7).

## 7.5. Criteria of inclusion and exclusion

An element is included in the collection if three conditions are simultaneously satisfied:

1. **Functional relevance** — the element realizes a function connected with the target function of the task, or reveals a mechanism useful for its solution.
2. **Demonstrable workability** — the element is a really working solution, not a hypothesis about a possible solution.
3. **Observability** — the element can be described and compared: it has a discernible mechanism of action.

An element is excluded if it fails at least one of the conditions. Two particular cases of exclusion are recorded separately: duplication (the element repeats what has already been collected) and clear incompatibility with the constraints of the task (for example, a contradiction with legal or ethical frameworks).

It is important that the criteria of inclusion and exclusion are applied identically to all elements, regardless of their origin. The researcher's preferences are not among the criteria.

## 7.6. Fixing the source and completeness

Each grain is accompanied by a record of origin: where the specimen was found, in what context, what function it realizes. This requirement serves two purposes.

The first is reproducibility: another researcher must be able to find the same source and check the extraction of the grain. The second is honesty: separating what was really found from what was conjectured by the researcher themselves.

Completeness of collection is understood not as the exhaustion of all possible solutions — this is in general unattainable — but as the coverage of the three directions of search and the reaching of saturation: the moment when new specimens stop adding new functions, merely repeating what has already been collected.

## 7.7. The criterion of completing collection

Collection is completed when all conditions are met:

- along each of the three directions no fewer than the established minimum of specimens has been accumulated;
- the discarded elements are recorded with reasons;
- for each grain the source is indicated;
- saturation is observed: the last added specimens opened no new functions, but repeated already-known ones.

Saturation is the main signal of completion. It means that the space of functions relevant to the task, within the chosen directions, has been examined sufficiently. Further search would bring duplicates, not new grains.

The result of the stage is not a text but the Grain Map — an ordered description of the collected elements through functions. Its construction is the subject of the next chapter.
