# CHAPTER 1. STATEMENT OF THE SCIENTIFIC PROBLEM AND RESEARCH PROGRAM

## 1.1. Relevance of the study

Every new piece of knowledge, device, product, or mode of organization grows out of something that came before. Behind any significant invention stands a long chain of prior observations, trials, errors, and findings, scattered across different fields and eras. The new emerges when the disparate is joined — deliberately, and not by chance.

From this grows one of the central questions of the theory of creativity and innovation: by what laws do elements of already-existing knowledge turn into a solution possessing new properties? And can this transition be not merely described after the fact, but organized — made transparent, reproducible, and open to verification?

Different traditions have answered this question in different ways. Schumpeter's economic theory explained development through "new combinations" of the means of production. The engineering school of TRIZ built a systematic search for solutions through contradictions and resources. The cognitive model Geneplore showed that creativity is an alternation of generating preliminary structures and then exploring them. Research on knowledge recombination has clarified how joining elements from different domains affects outcomes.

All these approaches contain parts of one answer, but they do not add up to a single working method. The open question remains: can search, functional description, analysis of compatibility, synthesis, and verification be assembled into one reproducible procedure?

This work attempts to build such a procedure. It rests on an authorial concept named the "Grain Principle", and its aim is not only to describe that principle, but to test it.

The relevance of this attempt is heightened in the age of artificial intelligence and large datasets. A machine can enumerate millions of sources, but abundance of information by itself guarantees neither the discovery of a useful link, nor a correct formulation of the task, nor a realizable result. What is needed is a methodological frame that directs collection, comprehension, and synthesis — and it is precisely the absence of such a frame that is felt most acutely today.

## 1.2. State of the problem

The problem of the emergence of the new is studied in several independent traditions, each supplying its own fragment of the future method.

**The economic tradition.** Schumpeter presented development as the realization of new combinations of resources, technologies, markets, and organizational forms. For us the essential thing is not the economic side but the viewpoint itself: innovation is not only the invention of a never-before-seen element, but also a new relation between already-existing elements.

**The engineering tradition.** TRIZ treats a technical task through system analysis, the identification of contradictions, and the search for solutions that overcome the limitations of the original system. Here what matters most is the emphasis on functions and contradictions: it permits comparing objects not by outward similarity but by mechanism of action.

**The cognitive tradition.** The Geneplore model (Finke, Ward, Smith) describes creativity as an interplay of generating preliminary structures and then exploring them. It reminds us that a creative result can arise not only from a pre-planned sequence, but also through the discovery of possibilities in initially indeterminate constructions.

**The knowledge-recombination tradition.** This line studies how joining elements from different domains affects scientific and technological performance. An important empirical finding is that "the farther apart, the better" does not hold. For example, F. Wang's study of pharmaceutical patents revealed a nonlinear relation between the cognitive distance of the joined knowledge and the value of inventions: excessive remoteness of components does not raise performance.

Thus, individual elements of the future method already exist and have been tested: new combinations, system analysis, functional representation, overcoming contradictions, generation and exploration of constructions, recombination of knowledge. But the existence of parts does not prove the existence of a whole. No tradition offers a reproducible procedure that unites all of the above into one process — from task definition to independent verification of the result. Building and testing such a unification constitutes the central task of this work.

## 1.3. The original concept: 集 — 合 — 生

The Grain Principle describes the creation of the new through three interrelated stages, denoted by three characters.

**集 (Collection).** The purposeful identification and accumulation of effective specimens — methods, technologies, ideas, solutions — relevant to the task under study. The search is not confined to one discipline or one historical period; it proceeds along three paths: deep into history, broadly across adjacent fields, and upward through abstract general principles.

**合 (Synthesis).** The analysis of what has been collected: identifying functions, principles of action, constraints, and possible links between elements. Here the transition occurs from the mere coexistence of findings to the construction of a holistic system.

**生 (Birth).** The formation and verification of a new solution that may possess properties and capabilities not reducible to the list of its source components.

The image of the grain expresses the essence: a grain carries the heredity of many generations, yet grows into an independent plant. The new is not obliged to negate the past — and must not remain its mechanical copy.

The grain metaphor is used as a conceptual model, not as proof of a biological or physical law. This must be stated at the outset: the image serves the navigation of thought, but replaces neither definition nor verification.

The original statement of the concept contained two positions that are retained in the present work.

The first is the **"blind bee" rule**. The researcher must collect not only what pleases them or confirms their expectations, but also effective solutions that provoke inner resistance. A bee does not choose a flower by taste; it gathers nectar.

The second is the **Grain Map**. The specimens found are described through the functions they perform — transportation, amplification, storage, transformation, feedback. Such description permits comparing objects that are outwardly dissimilar but realize one and the same function.

The present dissertation retains these positions while refining their scientific status, definitions, criteria, and procedure of application.

## 1.4. The scientific problem

The central problem of the study lies in a contradiction.

On the one hand, real innovation constantly uses the search, selection, transfer, and combination of existing knowledge. On the other hand, the very process of interdisciplinary synthesis remains weakly formalized: the researcher is forced each time to decide alone what to collect, how to compare, how to reveal functional links, and how to tell a promising construction from an arbitrary set of components.

From this contradiction grows the research question:

**Can a formalized integrative methodology be built that organizes interdisciplinary search, functional analysis, synthesis, and verification of new solutions, and that, under certain conditions, raises the productivity of the innovation process relative to less structured ways of working?**

The question is posed so as not to presuppose a positive answer. The study must allow that the methodology may prove useful only for certain classes of tasks, may require substantial limitations — or may not demonstrate advantages at all.

## 1.5. Object and subject of the study

**The object of the study** is the processes of creating new knowledge, technical solutions, products, and systems through the search, selection, transformation, and joining of previously existing elements.

**The subject of the study** is the principles, stages, tools, conditions, and criteria of an integrative methodology of interdisciplinary search and synthesis, formed on the basis of the Grain Principle.

A special methodological case is the application of the principle being developed to the creation of the dissertation itself: the work not only describes the method but is built by it, which makes it possible to observe the methodology in action.

## 1.6. Aim and objectives

**The aim** is to develop a theoretically grounded and practically testable methodology for creating new solutions on the basis of the Grain Principle, and to determine its conditions of applicability, its boundaries, and its criteria of effectiveness.

The objectives:

1. To analyze historical and modern approaches to the creation of the new.
2. To examine philosophical, systemic, and cognitive conceptions of the emergence of the new, separating conceptual analogies from verified mechanisms.
3. To identify what is common and what is different in existing methodologies of search and synthesis.
4. To give operational definitions of the basic notions of the Grain Principle.
5. To develop a procedure for the purposeful collection of solutions from historical, adjacent, and abstract domains.
6. To create a method of functional description and classification — the Grain Map.
7. To develop methods of decomposition, comparison, and assessment of the compatibility of elements.
8. To form a synthesis procedure aimed at creating a holistic system rather than a mechanical combination.
9. To define the criteria of novelty, usefulness, realizability, and stability of the result.
10. To develop a protocol for the experimental verification of the methodology and for comparison with alternatives.
11. To investigate the role of artificial intelligence as a tool of search, analysis, and documentation.
12. To apply the methodology to the practical tasks of SHIKARDOS.
13. To document the reflexive application of the principle to the creation of the dissertation itself.

## 1.7. Working hypothesis

**If the process of creating a new solution is organized as a sequence of purposeful interdisciplinary search, functional description, analysis of compatibility and contradictions, holistic synthesis, and independent verification, then for certain classes of tasks such an organization will raise the transparency, reproducibility, and productivity of the search relative to unstructured idea generation.**

What matters is not the quantity of collected elements but the quality of their selection and analysis. The conditions on which this depends:

- a clear statement of the initial task;
- diversity of sources together with their relevance;
- description of elements through functions and mechanisms;
- identification of constraints and contradictions;
- justification of the links between components;
- testable criteria of the quality of the result;
- the possibility of independent evaluation and reproduction.

The hypothesis yields testable propositions: (a) functional description eases the discovery of links between heterogeneous objects; (b) systematic search along several directions increases the diversity of options; (c) analysis of compatibility cuts off unproductive combinations before costly realization; (d) independent verification distinguishes the subjective sense of novelty from confirmed value. All these propositions are research positions requiring verification, not established facts.

## 1.8. Methodological basis

The work is built at the intersection of approaches: system analysis (a solution as a wholeness of interrelated elements), comparative analysis, functional analysis, historical-scientific analysis, interdisciplinary search, modeling, experimental-comparative study, and expert evaluation.

The organizing frame is the triad 集 — 合 — 生, supplemented by two obligatory contours: the statement of the task before collection, and the verification of the result after birth. These contours do not replace the triad; they create the conditions of its application.

The working cycle:

**Task definition → 集 (Collection) → Grain Map → 合 (Synthesis) → 生 (Birth) → Verification → feedback and a new cycle.**

Verification is not an external add-on to creativity but a condition of recognizing the result as achieved. A solution that fails verification returns to the previous stages for a revision of the initial assumptions, the composition of elements, or the manner of their joining.

## 1.9. Reflexive application of the Grain Principle

The dissertation not only describes the Grain Principle but is created with its help. At the stage of collection a corpus of sources and historical examples is formed; at the stage of synthesis the common mechanisms and constraints are identified; at the stage of birth one's own definitions, model, and criteria are formulated. This is designated as the reflexive application of the principle: the methodology is at once the subject of the study and the instrument of organizing the work.

A strict limitation is required here: applying a method to itself does not prove its truth. Otherwise a logical circle arises — the method is declared successful because a result was obtained by it, and the result is successful because it was obtained by this method.

To exclude the circle it is necessary: to keep a journal of the origin of ideas and decisions; to record not only successful but also failed attempts; to separate assumptions from results of verification; to preserve alternative explanations; to use external evaluation; and to compare the result with the results of other methodologies on comparable tasks.

Two levels of proof are distinguished. The first is the application of the principle to the creation of the dissertation itself: it documents the formation of the methodology. The second is application to independent tasks: only this evaluates the usefulness of the method beyond the text. Success at the first level does not mean success at the second.

## 1.10. Criteria of effectiveness

To test the hypothesis a multicriteria system is used, refined in later chapters:

1. **Novelty** — the difference from known analogues, assessed by the available information rather than by the author's subjective impression.
2. **Functional usefulness** — the ability to perform the task or to improve measurable characteristics.
3. **Systemic wholeness** — the justification of links and the absence of critical contradictions.
4. **Realizability** — the possibility of embodiment given technological, economic, resource, legal, and organizational constraints.
5. **Reproducibility** — the possibility of repeating the procedure and obtaining a comparable result.
6. **Productivity of development** — the ability of the solution to open new functions and directions.
7. **Resource efficiency** — the relation of the result to the costs.

No single criterion proves success: what is original may be unrealizable, and what is useful may lack novelty. Hence the evaluation is multicriterial, and the weights of criteria are set before comparison, as far as possible.

## 1.11. Scientific novelty

Novelty cannot be established by an authorial claim — only by the analysis of the literature and by verification. As presumed results that may claim novelty, the following are considered:

1. An integrative model uniting collection, functional representation, analysis of compatibility, synthesis, and verification.
2. A formalization of the notion of the "grain" as a functionally significant element of a solution.
3. The structure of the "Grain Map" and the rules of its application.
4. A procedure for the transition from a set of elements to a holistic synthesis.
5. Criteria of evaluation and a protocol of experimental comparison.
6. The justification and documentation of the reflexive application.

Each point is checked for analogues; if an analogue already exists, it is not declared a discovery — the contribution then consists in the refinement, integration, or verification of the known.

## 1.12. Practical significance

The Grain Principle is intended for use as an organized procedure for developing and perfecting products, technologies, and organizational systems. Within SHIKARDOS the methodology is applicable to the analysis of existing solutions, the search for functional analogues, the formation of product concepts, and the assessment of realizability.

A separate direction is artificial intelligence as a tool for searching sources, classifying elements, building maps of links, and preparing materials. AI is treated as an instrument, not a source of truth: its answers are subject to verification.

The practical result is not the description of a philosophy but usable instruments: regulations, templates, algorithms, maps, protocols, and criteria.

## 1.13. Boundaries and limitations

The work does not aim to prove that the Grain Principle is a universal law of nature, history, or creativity. The similarity between the stages of the methodology and processes described in biology, physics, or philosophy does not by itself mean the identity of their mechanisms.

It is not assumed that any combination of elements engenders a new quality: a combination may be incompatible, redundant, or already known. The notion of the "best specimen" is defined relative to a concrete task; a universally best solution outside a context may not exist. The notion of "new quality" requires operationalization — a link to observable functions and properties, not to a metaphor.

The effectiveness of the methodology depends on the domain, the researcher's qualification, the availability of data, and resources; therefore the conclusions are limited to the conditions of verification.

## 1.14. Structure of the study

The work consists of fourteen chapters in four parts.

**Part I. Foundations.** Chapter 1 — statement of the problem; Chapter 2 — historical roots of the methods of creating the new; Chapter 3 — philosophical foundations of synthesis and of new quality; Chapter 4 — systemic, evolutionary, and cognitive models.

**Part II. The modern environment.** Chapter 5 — modern methods of search and recombination; Chapter 6 — the theoretical model of the Grain Principle.

**Part III. Methodology.** Chapter 7 — formalization of Collection; Chapter 8 — the Grain Map; Chapter 9 — formalization of Synthesis; Chapter 10 — formalization of Birth; Chapter 11 — evaluation and verification.

**Part IV. Application.** Chapter 12 — software implementation and AI; Chapter 13 — application to SHIKARDOS; Chapter 14 — reflexivity, limitations, ethics, prospects.

## Conclusions to Chapter 1

The scientific problem is formulated: whether interdisciplinary search and synthesis can be systematized into a reproducible procedure. It is shown that the individual components of the methodology have foundations in existing traditions, but their unification into one process and its verification remain the task of this work.

The object, subject, aim, objectives, and hypothesis are defined. The original triad 集 — 合 — 生 is preserved as the core and supplemented by task definition, the Grain Map, and obligatory verification. A special place is held by the reflexive application, which documents the formation of the methodology but does not serve as proof of its effectiveness.

The first chapter sets the boundaries and the program of verification. The subsequent chapters answer three questions: what foundations already exist, what new the Grain Principle offers, and whether its practical value is confirmed.
