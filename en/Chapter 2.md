# CHAPTER 2. HISTORICAL ROOTS OF THE METHODS OF CREATING THE NEW

## 2.1. Craft: knowledge accumulated in the hands

Long before science and engineering appeared, people created the new within the craft tradition. The master forged, potted, and built, and their knowledge was not written in formulas — it lived in the hands, in techniques, in the passing from teacher to apprentice.

Yet craft already contained the first features of the future method. The master observed the properties of material, remembered successful solutions and mistakes, and accumulated techniques passed to the next generation. This was a slow, spontaneous analogue of collection and selection: the effective survived, the ineffective died out. The grain of the method — "take the best of what has accumulated and pass it on" — is present here in its first, unconscious form.

It is important, however, that craft knowledge was tied to a concrete master and a concrete object. It was rarely generalized and even more rarely transferred from one field to another. A glassblower's secret did not help a blacksmith; a mason's technique did not reach a carpenter. What was missing was exactly what would become the core of the Grain Principle: the translation of a solution from the level of the thing to the level of the function.

## 2.2. Antiquity: the first attempts at generalization

Ancient thought took a decisive step — it tried to extract general rules from practice. Greek geometry reduced the measurement of land to theorems applicable to any triangle, not only to a particular field. Roman engineers systematized the construction of roads and aqueducts into standard solutions reproducible across different provinces.

Here, for the first time, a principle appeared that would become central: knowledge, separated from its concrete carrier, can be transferred and applied anew. Abstraction — a geometric theorem, a standard construction — proved to be a form of storing function without tying it to material.

However, ancient systematization covered mostly already-known areas. The transfer of principles between far-distant spheres — say, from living nature to technology — remained the exception rather than the method.

## 2.3. Alchemy and the Renaissance: from secrecy to method

Medieval alchemy, despite its reputation, was one of the first attempts at the systematic study of the transformations of substance. The alchemist did not merely await inspiration — they kept records, repeated experiments, accumulated observations. Their laboratory journal was the embryo of the scientific protocol.

In parallel, in the Renaissance craft workshops an unnoticed but important shift occurred: workshops began to join knowledge from different sources. Leonardo da Vinci studied the flight of birds, the movement of water, and human anatomy not as separate subjects but as manifestations of common natural laws that could be carried into an engineering solution. His notebooks are an early example of what would later be called interdisciplinary search.

The common feature of the era: knowledge ceased to be a closed secret of a guild and became the subject of accumulation and comparison. But the process still depended on the genius of an individual and was not reproducible.

## 2.4. The scientific revolution: method as a public procedure

The scientific revolution of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries accomplished a turning point: it turned the search for truth from a personal art into a public procedure. Francis Bacon formulated the demand for the systematic collection of facts and inductive generalization, opposing it to reliance on authority and chance inspiration. Galileo and Newton showed that observation, measurement, and mathematical formulation yield results that another person can check and reproduce.

Two consequences matter here for our topic. First, the verifiability of a result became a condition of its recognition — an idea the Grain Principle places at the center. Second, knowledge acquired a form permitting accumulation and transmission independent of the personality of the creator. The experience of one became available to all.

Yet the scientific method of that era aimed at explaining an already-existing world, not at the purposeful creation of the new. Invention remained on the other side — closer to craft than to science.

## 2.5. The industrial revolution: invention as a profession

The industrial revolution of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries produced a new type of actor — the inventor, for whom the creation of the new became the main occupation. The patent system that emerged in this period formalized novelty: it required fixing the solution, distinguishing it from known ones, and describing it publicly.

This was an important shift toward the Grain Principle. A patent is, in essence, a grain map: it describes not only the outward form of a device but its purpose, its principle of action, and its difference from predecessors. The experience of inventors — Watt, Stephenson, Edison — showed that significant solutions often arose as the transfer of a principle from one field to another: the steam engine joined knowledge of heat, mechanics, and pumps.

At the same time, invention remained a matter of individual intuition. Edison's laboratory systematized search through the enumeration of variants but did not yield a general method that could be taught.

## 2.6. The twentieth century: the systematization of invention

In the twentieth century, the first attempts appeared to turn invention into a teachable discipline. The most significant of them is TRIZ (the theory of inventive problem solving), created by Genrich Altshuller and his colleagues.

TRIZ proceeds from the analysis of tens of thousands of patents and identifies recurring regularities in them. Its key ideas directly echo the Grain Principle: a solution is described through functions and contradictions, not outward form; search is conducted systematically; there exists a set of generalized techniques applicable to different tasks. TRIZ first showed that invention can be largely formalized.

By the end of the century, cognitive studies of creativity (including the Geneplore model) added to this, describing the creative process as an alternation of generation and exploration of structures, and studies of knowledge recombination, showing that innovation is often born from the joining of previously scattered elements.

## 2.7. Conclusion: from spontaneous accumulation to method

The historical line traced in this chapter reveals a stable direction of movement. Humanity went successively from the spontaneous accumulation of techniques — through the first generalizations — to a public, verifiable procedure of cognition — and, finally, to attempts to formalize the very creation of the new.

Each stage added to the method one of its future parts, but none assembled them into a whole. Craft gave accumulation and selection. Antiquity gave abstraction and transfer. Science gave verifiability and publicity. TRIZ gave functional description and systematic search. Cognitive studies gave the understanding of the process of generation.

What is missing is precisely the integration: a procedure that would bind collection, functional description, analysis of compatibility, synthesis, and verification into one reproducible cycle. The construction of such a procedure is the subject of this work.
