# CHAPTER 12. SOFTWARE IMPLEMENTATION AND THE USE OF ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE IN THE GRAIN PRINCIPLE

## 12.1. The place of software tools in the method

The Grain Principle is a methodology, not a program. Its core — the sequence of stages and criteria — does not depend on whether the work is performed manually or with a machine. However, in practice the volume of collection, description, and comparison is such that software tools become not a luxury but a condition of scalability.

Software implementation therefore does not replace the method but serves it. The machine takes on what is formalized: searching for sources, extracting descriptions, building maps, generating variants. The person retains what is not formalized: the statement of the task, the judgment of compatibility, the choice of form, the responsibility for the result.

Such a division of labor sets the frame for the whole chapter: for each stage it is indicated what can be automated and what must remain with the researcher.

## 12.2. The automation of collection

The collection stage is the most natural object of automation. Large language models and search tools can quickly find solutions of a given function in vast arrays of texts, patents, and documentation.

Automated collection solves three tasks. The first is the widening of coverage: the machine looks through volumes inaccessible to manual reading. The second is the identification of candidates: from the array are selected fragments potentially relevant to the target function. The third is the extraction of a primary description: for each candidate a draft of the functional record is formed.

However, the automation of collection runs into a principled boundary. The machine does not answer for the relevance and reliability of what is found. The blind-bee rule requires the researcher to collect also what provokes resistance; a machine trained on plausible answers tends to confirm expectations, not to break them. Therefore the result of automatic collection is subject to manual verification: each source is verified, each extracted description is checked against the original.

## 12.3. The automation of the Grain Map

The Grain Map requires the translation of descriptions from the language of things into the language of functions. This operation is partially formalizable and hence partially automatable.

The machine can propose a classification: assign a grain to a function class, formulate the function and the mechanism in a first approximation, and group close records. For large maps such preliminary marking substantially accelerates the work.

But a functional description is an intellectual act, not a mechanical transformation. The machine can suggest a function, but it cannot guarantee that the suggested function corresponds to the real mechanism of the solution. Therefore each record of the map proposed by the machine is confirmed by a person. Automation here is a draft, not a result.

The format of the map introduced in Chapter 8 is deliberately fit for machine processing: the fixed fields admit storage, search, and comparison. But fitness for processing does not mean reliability of content; it means only convenience of working with what has already been verified by a person.

## 12.4. The automation of synthesis

Synthesis is automated to the least degree, and this is principled, not technical.

The machine is useful as an assistant: it generates variants of structures, highlights potential contradictions, checks the completeness of the coverage of required functions, and builds graphs of links. These operations are formal and lend themselves well to automation.

But the decision of which structure is holistic and which is a mechanical combination remains with the person. The criterion of emergent quality, introduced in Chapter 9, requires a judgment of whether a new property has arisen; the machine has no ground for such a judgment, although it can provide the data confirming or refuting it.

Thus the automation of synthesis has the character of support: the machine widens the number of considered variants and accelerates the check of formal conditions, but the choice and the responsibility are not transferred to it.

## 12.5. Artificial intelligence as a tool, not a source of truth

Through all the stages runs one premise: artificial intelligence is a tool of research activity, not a source of truth.

This means three practical rules. The first is the verification of sources: the factual claims produced by the machine are checked against primary sources. The second is the separation of fact and generation: text produced by the machine is not considered proof merely because it is plausible. The third is the recording of the role: the documentation indicates what exactly was done by the machine and what by the person.

This premise is especially important in a context where plausibility is easily taken for reliability. The machine produces coherent and confident text regardless of its correspondence to reality; the method is obliged to place between this text and the conclusion a procedure of verification.

The Grain Principle proves here not weakened but strengthened: its verification contour — the independent evaluation of the result — is precisely the mechanism that protects against the uncritical acceptance of machine answers.

## 12.6. The boundaries of automation

Summarizing, one can fix the boundary of automation for each stage.

Fully or to a significant degree automated are the formal operations: search over arrays, primary extraction, preliminary classification, generation of variants, the check of completeness and formal consistency.

Not automated are the operations of judgment: the statement of the task, the choice of function, the assessment of compatibility, the recognition of emergent quality, the responsibility for the result.

This boundary is not a temporary technical limitation that will disappear with the development of technology. It follows from the very definition of the method: the Grain Principle is a procedure of responsible creativity, and responsibility by definition cannot be delegated to a tool.
