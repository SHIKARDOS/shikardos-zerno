# CHAPTER 11. A MODEL FOR EVALUATION, EXPERIMENTAL VERIFICATION, AND DEVELOPMENT OF RESULTS

## 11.1. The purpose of evaluation and verification

Verification is the stage separating the claimed from the confirmed. Birth gives a solution and records its new quality, but this is the author's record. Scientific recognition requires that the solution be evaluated by pre-set criteria and, where possible, compared with alternatives.

Verification solves two tasks. The first is to establish whether the solution performs the set task and possesses the claimed quality. The second is to evaluate whether the method itself is justified: whether the Grain Principle brings an advantage over less structured ways of working.

Verification is separated from synthesis in time and in performers. The author of a solution must not be its only evaluator; where possible, independent expertise is brought in. This requirement goes back to the rule of separating generation and exploration, fixed in Chapter 4.

## 11.2. The system of criteria: elaboration

Evaluation is conducted by the seven criteria introduced in Chapter 1. Here they are elaborated in operational form.

**Novelty.** Established by comparison with known analogues from available sources. It is recorded how the solution differs and whether the difference is confirmed by documents. Novelty is not declared but established relative to the corpus of preceding solutions.

**Functional usefulness.** The solution must perform the target function. Usefulness is measured where the function admits measurement — by time, quality, cost — and described qualitatively where measurement is difficult.

**Systemic wholeness.** The soundness of the links and the absence of critical contradictions are checked. The criterion rests on the record of the structure made at the stage of synthesis.

**Realizability.** The possibility of embodiment is assessed given technological, economic, resource, legal, and organizational constraints. A solution not realizable under the given conditions fails verification regardless of its other merits.

**Reproducibility.** Checked by the possibility of repeating the procedure and obtaining a comparable result. Reproducibility rests on the completeness of documentation: the design, sources, selection, structure, and embodiment must be recorded.

**Productivity of development.** It is assessed whether the solution opens new functions, variants of application, or directions. The criterion matters because the value of a solution is often not exhausted by its immediate purpose.

**Resource efficiency.** Relates the result to the costs of time, means, and effort. Efficiency is assessed not in absolute terms but in comparison with alternative paths to the same result.

No criterion is sufficient by itself. The evaluation is multicriterial, and the weights of the criteria are set before the comparison of results, as far as possible — otherwise the weights risk being fitted to the desired conclusion.

## 11.3. The protocol of experimental verification

To test the effectiveness of the method itself a comparative protocol is used.

A task admitting solution by several ways is formulated. It is solved by two paths: with the application of the Grain Principle and with the application of an alternative approach — for example, unstructured idea generation or an existing engineering method. Then both results are evaluated by one and the same system of criteria.

The correctness of the comparison is ensured by several conditions. The task for both paths must be one and the same. The evaluation must be carried out by those who do not know by which path the result was obtained — as far as practically achievable. The criteria and their weights are fixed before the comparison begins. Negative results are recorded on a par with positive ones.

The protocol does not assume that the Grain Principle will prove better in all cases. The hypothesis is formulated cautiously: an advantage is expected only for certain classes of tasks. Therefore the protocol includes the recording of the class of the task, so that conclusions can be limited to the corresponding conditions.

## 11.4. Comparison with alternative methods

The choice of an alternative for comparison depends on the class of the task. For engineering tasks a comparison with systematic design is appropriate; for product tasks, with design thinking; for tasks demanding novelty, with unstructured generation.

Comparison is conducted not for the victory of one method over another, but for establishing the boundaries of applicability. The value of the research result lies in indicating where the Grain Principle gives an advantage, where it is neutral, and where it loses. Such mapping is more honest and more useful than the assertion of universal superiority.

It is essential that comparison may reveal the absence of advantage as well. This outcome does not devalue the work: it refines its boundaries and is a full-fledged scientific result.

## 11.5. Feedback and a new cycle

Verification does not finish the process but opens it into a new cycle. The result of verification returns to the corresponding stage.

If the solution does not perform the target function — the structure of synthesis is revised. If no suitable form was found — a return to the birth stage. If a shortage of functions is revealed — a return to collection. If the new quality was not confirmed — the design itself is revised.

Feedback turns the Grain Principle from a linear sequence into a closed cycle, and in this lies the source of its stability. An error at any stage does not discard the whole work but is localized and corrected where it arose.

Journaling plays a key role here: only the recorded decisions of the stages permit indicating precisely where to return. Without a journal the cycle degenerates into chaotic repetition.

## 11.6. The development of the result: productivity

Verification takes into account not only the immediate result but also its productivity — the ability to open further directions.

The born solution itself becomes a grain. It replenishes the Grain Map and enters the collection of future tasks. Thus the Grain Principle reproduces the logic of inheritance: each solution is not a final point but a link in a chain, from which the next ones grow.

This moment brings the method closer to the evolutionary logic noted in Chapter 4, but with a substantial difference: inheritance here is purposeful. A solution is passed on not spontaneously but through its explicit inclusion in the map, with recorded function, mechanism, and constraints.

The evaluation of productivity therefore has a double meaning: it measures the value of the solution and at the same time prepares material for future cycles. A solution productive in development is more valuable than a solution exhausted by one application — even if by the remaining criteria they are equal.
