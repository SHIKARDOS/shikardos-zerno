# CHAPTER 4. SYSTEMIC, EVOLUTIONARY, AND COGNITIVE MODELS OF NEW STRUCTURE FORMATION

## 4.1. The system approach: structure, links, wholeness

The system approach treats an object not as an isolated thing but as a set of interrelated elements forming a wholeness that behaves differently from a simple sum of parts. Three notions form its core: element, link, and structure.

For the Grain Principle the system view yields an important clarification. The elements collected at the 集 stage have no independent meaning outside the links into which they will be placed. One and the same element — a wheel, a membrane, an algorithm — performs different functions and yields different results in different structures. Therefore synthesis (合) is work with links above all, and not with the elements themselves: it is the building of relations that turns a set into a system.

The system approach warns against two opposite errors. The first is to reduce the whole to the sum of its parts and to miss emergent properties. The second is to declare wholeness a mystical quality independent of composition. The truth lies in between: wholeness arises from a concrete composition and concrete links, and the task of the method is to make this transition observable and reproducible.

Hence a practical requirement for the methodology: at the stage of synthesis one must explicitly record which elements are joined, by which links, and for the sake of which function. Without such recording, the birth of the new remains inexplicable both to the researcher and to the verifier.

## 4.2. The evolutionary analogy: variation, selection, inheritance

Evolutionary theory describes the emergence of new forms through three mechanisms: variation (the appearance of diversity), selection (the preservation of the viable), and inheritance (the passing on of the selected). This triad is so universal that it is applied not only to biology but also to the evolution of technologies, ideas, and organizations.

The resemblance to the Grain Principle is evident, and it should not be understated. Collection (集) is the purposeful creation of variation — not random, but gathered from the best specimens. Synthesis and verification (合 and the verification contour) perform the role of selection — of the many possible combinations, the viable survives. And the born solution becomes an element of future collection — inheritance closes the cycle.

A strict distinction is necessary, however. Evolution is blind: it enumerates variants through a vast number of trials and has no goal. The Grain Principle, by contrast, is purposeful: it begins with a task definition and consciously selects elements for its solution. Therefore the borrowing from evolution is limited to the very logic of "variation — selection — inheritance", and not to its mechanism of enumeration. The method saves what evolution spends in abundance — time and the number of failed trials.

In addition, the evolutionary analogy reminds us of the importance of diversity at the stage of collection: without sufficient variation, selection has nothing to choose from. The "blind bee" rule — gathering even what provokes resistance — follows directly from this logic.

## 4.3. Cognitive models of creativity: generation and exploration

Cognitive psychology describes the creative process from within. The Geneplore model (R. Finke, T. Ward, S. Smith) represents it as an alternation of two phases: the generation of preliminary structures and their subsequent exploration. First the mind creates something indeterminate — a sketch, a hypothesis, a draft — then studies it, discovering in it properties and possibilities absent from the original plan.

Two consequences matter for the Grain Principle. The first: a creative result need not be fully predetermined at the start. A construction generated at the synthesis stage may reveal unexpected properties discovered only in exploration — this corresponds to the verification contour, where the solution is tested and, if necessary, returned for revision.

The second consequence: generation and exploration must be separated in time. Mixing them — when the researcher simultaneously invents and criticizes — suppresses creativity. The Grain Principle institutionalizes this separation: collection and synthesis are not accompanied by premature evaluation, and evaluation is carried out as a separate stage.

## 4.4. The unity of the models and their limitations

The three approaches considered — the system, the evolutionary, and the cognitive — describe one and the same process of the emergence of the new from three sides: as the structure of the whole, as the mechanism of selection, and as the inner experience of creativity. Their unity supports the basic premise of the Grain Principle: the emergence of the new is an organized, and not an arbitrary, process.

But this unity must not pass into identification. Each model is a simplification, and none is proof of the method's workability. The system model explains structure but does not guarantee that synthesis will lead to an emergent property. The evolutionary analogy suggests a logic but does not replace verification. The cognitive model describes the process but does not prescribe how to organize it reproducibly.

Scientific caution requires treating these models as orientations, not as a justification of truth. The confirmation or refutation of the methodology lies in the empirical part of the work, not in the philosophical part.

## 4.5. Conclusion: three models — three facets of one process

The system approach gives the methodology the language of structure: elements, links, wholeness. The evolutionary analogy gives it a logic: variation, selection, inheritance. The cognitive model gives it a procedure: generation and exploration.

Together they form the background against which the triad 集 — 合 — 生 takes on the shape of a full method: collection creates variation and supplies potencies; synthesis builds links and structure; birth and verification select the viable and record the emergent quality.

The following chapters pass from foundations to construction. Part II will consider the modern scientific-technological environment and formulate the theoretical model of the Grain Principle, delimiting it from the approaches considered.
