# CHAPTER 6. THE THEORETICAL MODEL OF THE GRAIN PRINCIPLE AND ITS DIFFERENCE FROM EXISTING APPROACHES

## 6.1. The full cycle of the Grain Principle

The Grain Principle is an integrative methodology for creating new solutions, organized as a closed cycle of seven steps:

**Task definition → 集 (Collection) → Grain Map → 合 (Synthesis) → 生 (Birth) → Verification → feedback.**

The three central stages — 集, 合, 生 — form the core of the method. Two outer contours — task definition and verification — frame the core and turn it from a creative impulse into a reproducible procedure.

**Task definition** fixes which function or which contradiction is to be resolved. Without it, collection is aimless: one cannot collect "everything useful in general", one can collect only for a concrete task.

**集 (Collection)** is the purposeful identification of effective solutions from three directions: historical (how the task was solved before), horizontal (how similar functions are solved in adjacent fields), and abstract (which general principles stand behind what has been found). Collection obeys the "blind bee" rule: what provokes resistance is accepted as well.

**The Grain Map** is an intermediate but obligatory step: the collected solutions are translated from the language of things into the language of functions. Each found solution is described not by how it looks but by what function it performs and by which mechanism.

**合 (Synthesis)** is the building of a holistic system from the functional elements. Here compatibility, contradictions, and constraints are revealed; combinations incapable of forming a workable whole are cut off.

**生 (Birth)** is the formation of a concrete solution, the embodiment of the synthesized structure in a material or symbolic form possessing testable properties.

**Verification** is the independent testing of the result by the criteria of novelty, usefulness, wholeness, realizability, reproducibility, productivity, and resource efficiency. A solution that fails verification returns to the corresponding stage — the cycle repeats.

## 6.2. Operational definitions of the basic notions

For the reproducibility of the method it is necessary to define its key notions operationally, that is, through observable operations.

**Grain** — a functionally significant element of a solution that can be isolated, described through the function it performs, and compared with elements from other domains. A grain is not a thing and not an idea as such, but a function considered apart from its concrete carrier.

**Collection** — the procedure of identifying and fixing grains relevant to the task, with the source of each element indicated.

**Grain Map** — an ordered description of the collected elements through their functions, mechanisms of action, and constraints, permitting the comparison of heterogeneous solutions.

**Synthesis** — the building from grains of a new structure in which the links between elements are set explicitly and justified by the function of the whole.

**Birth** — the obtaining of a solution possessing at least one property or function not reducible to the list of source grains, and admitting verification.

**Verification** — an independent evaluation of the solution by pre-set criteria, separated in time from synthesis.

**Blind bee** — a rule of collection prescribing the acceptance of solutions regardless of their aesthetic or ideological appeal to the researcher.

These definitions are deliberately minimal. They do not predetermine which exact tools will be applied at each stage — that is the subject of the following chapters, where the methodology is formalized in detail.

## 6.3. Difference from TRIZ

TRIZ and the Grain Principle share a functional view of the solution and the systematic character of search. Both refuse to describe objects merely by outward form, and both recognize the role of contradictions.

The difference lies in the organization of the process. TRIZ is focused on solving a technical contradiction inside an already-outlined task; its strength is in the tools of analysis and transformation of a concrete system. The Grain Principle emphasizes an earlier stage — the purposeful interdisciplinary collection and the building of a functional map — and also a later one — the independent verification of the result and its return to the cycle.

In other words, TRIZ answers chiefly the question "how to transform a system", while the Grain Principle answers "from what, and by what process, to create a system that does not yet exist." These questions are not opposed but complementary; the methodological novelty of the Grain Principle lies in binding collection and verification into one cycle, which TRIZ does not do in explicit form.

## 6.4. Difference from design thinking and generation methods

Design thinking builds an iterative cycle with verification through prototyping, which outwardly resembles the Grain Principle. Generation methods (brainstorming, morphological analysis) are strong in producing options.

The principal difference is in the source of material. Design thinking and generative techniques rely first of all on the imagination of the participants and on user interviews. The Grain Principle places at the start the systematic collection of existing effective solutions: before inventing, one should study what has already been invented and how.

A second difference follows. In generative methods the quality of the result depends strongly on the composition and talent of the participants. The Grain Principle seeks to lower this dependence: collection and the functional map provide material open to verification and reproduction regardless of who exactly performs the work.

Finally, design thinking is oriented toward user needs and the aesthetics of the product; the Grain Principle is oriented toward function and mechanism. These are different, though intersecting, aims.

## 6.5. Difference from knowledge recombination and cognitive models

Research on knowledge recombination describes a phenomenon — how the joining of elements engenders the new. The Grain Principle turns this phenomenon into a prescription: it sets a procedure by which such joining can be organized. It is one thing to establish that recombination is useful; another to indicate how exactly to conduct it.

The difference from cognitive models (Geneplore and similar) is analogous. A cognitive model describes the creative process from within — generation and exploration; the Grain Principle institutionalizes this separation in a procedure: synthesis and verification are divided into different stages so that premature criticism does not suppress generation.

Thus the Grain Principle does not discover anew any of the named phenomena. Its contribution is integration: bringing scattered discoveries into one reproducible sequence.

## 6.6. Conclusion: the place of the Grain Principle

The theoretical model of the Grain Principle occupies an empty place in the space of existing approaches. It does not replace TRIZ, design thinking, engineering design, or knowledge recombination — it binds their fragments into a single cycle: task definition, collection, functional map, synthesis, birth, verification.

The central notion of the model is the grain — a function separated from its carrier. It is this notion that permits comparing heterogeneous solutions and transferring principles between domains, which constitutes the essence of interdisciplinary synthesis.

The following chapters pass from theory to construction: they formalize the stage of collection (Chapter 7), build the Grain Map (Chapter 8), formalize synthesis (Chapter 9) and birth (Chapter 10), and then evaluation and verification (Chapter 11).
